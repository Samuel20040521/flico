🛠️ Python 相關工具包推薦
1. 核心模擬引擎：PySpice
這是 Python 中最主流的電路模擬包。它本質上是 Lycée 或 Ngspice 的 Python 接口。

功能：進行直流（DC）、交流（AC）及瞬態分析。

MCP6004 與 BPW34：這類特定元件需要 SPICE 模型（.mod 或 .lib 檔）。Microchip 官方有提供 MCP6001/2/4 的 SPICE Macro Models，你可以將其導入 PySpice 中進行精確仿真。  
PDF

優點：計算精確，支持複雜的半導體特性。

2. 電路圖輸出：SchemDraw
如果你想要用 Python 代碼生成高品質的電路圖（如論文或文檔使用的那種），這是首選。

功能：利用代碼定義元件連接，輸出為向量圖（SVG/PDF）或 PNG。

限制：它只負責「畫圖」，不具備物理模擬功能。你必須手動定義 MCP6004 的接腳位置。

3. 麵包板視覺化：目前 Python 的缺口
這是最難的部分。 Python 目前沒有成熟的自動生成「麵包板視覺效果圖」的包。

現狀：大多數開發者會使用 Fritzing 手動繪製麵包板圖。

Python 替代方案：你可能需要利用 matplotlib 或 PIL/Pillow 自己編寫腳本，將元件的圖像座標映射到麵包板底圖上，這屬於半手動開發。

### 3. 💡 你將在圖表上看到的物理現象 (提前劇透)
在轉阻放大器（TIA）中，理想的輸出電壓公式是：**$V_{OUT} = I_{photo} \times R_F$**。但因為你的電源 $V_{DD}$ 只有 **3.3V**，這代表放大器的輸出「絕對不可能」超過 3.3V，這就是所謂的**削頂（Clipping）/ 飽和現象**。

程式跑完後，你將會在圖表上看到這四條線的趨勢：
1.  **$R_F = 1~M\Omega$（高增益）**：光電流才剛到大約 3.3 µA，輸出電壓就撞到 3.3V 天花板了，後面的線會變成一條平坦的水平線（飽和）。
2.  **$R_F = 100~k\Omega$**：光電流到達約 33 µA 時，輸出電壓撞到 3.3V 天花板。
3.  **$R_F = 10~k\Omega$（中增益）**：在 50 µA 的光電流下，輸出電壓最高只到 0.5V，整條線都是完美的斜直線，**沒有飽和**。
4.  **$R_F = 1~k\Omega$（低增益）**：在 50 µA 下，輸出僅 50 mV，線太平緩了，可能會被雜訊淹沒。

> **關於回授電容 $C_F$ (100nF)**：
> 在這個「直流（DC）掃描」中，電容等同於斷路，所以它不會影響這張圖表的結果。但 Claude 已經幫你把它接上去了，這非常棒，因為你未來如果要測量「光閃爍的頻率反應（AC 掃描）」，這個 $C_F$ 就會決定你的電路頻寬與穩定度！

目前還有什麼問題？

這是一個在使用 PySpice 和原廠 SPICE 模型時非常經典的問題！問題出在 Ngspice 模擬器的相容性與初始化設定。

讓我白話解釋一下發生了什麼事：

🚨 發生了什麼問題？
原廠模型的語法太舊/太特規：Microchip 提供的 MCP6004 模型檔案中，使用了大量的 POLY 和 TABLE 語法。這是早期 PSpice（另一套商業模擬軟體）特有的寫法。

Ngspice 不認得這語法：你安裝的底層引擎是開源的 Ngspice。預設情況下，Ngspice 看不懂 POLY，它會嘗試啟動它的「XSPICE 相容模式」來翻譯這些舊語法。

缺少翻譯字典：你剛剛只安裝了 libngspice0（純核心運算庫），但沒有安裝 spinit 初始化檔與 .cm（XSPICE CodeModels）擴充模組。這就像是請了一個會算微積分的人，但他忘了帶微積分公式表出門。

結果就是：Ngspice 看到原廠模型裡的 POLY 語法後，因為找不到對應的 .cm 模組，直接報錯當機（MIF-ERROR - unable to find definition of model）。

🛠️ 解決方案 (二選一)
Claude 給了你兩個選項，我強烈建議選擇 Path A。

推薦方案：補齊 Ngspice 完整套件 (Path A)
這是最乾淨、最正確的做法。因為原廠模型（Microchip 寫的）是最權威的，我們不應該去亂改它，而是應該把模擬器的環境設定好。

👉 你的動作：

不推薦方案：手動修改原廠模型 (Path B)
這條路是讓 Claude 寫程式去把 MCP6004 模型檔裡面的 POLY 語法，一行一行翻譯成 Ngspice 原生支援的 B-source 寫法。

缺點：非常危險！原廠模型是用來保證精確度的，隨便用腳本替換裡面的數學方程式，極有可能導致模擬失真（例如電流消耗不對、頻率響應跑掉）。Claude 也警告說這會 "subtly distorting Microchip's published behavior"。

## 2. Saturation / Clipping Analysis



Every cell that reads **3.33 V** is the rail. The MCP6004 is a rail-to-rail-output CMOS op-amp, so its output cannot exceed V_DD; the bench measurement of 3.33 V tracks the simulated 3.3 V clipping ceiling almost exactly (the extra 30 mV is well within the meter's quoted accuracy and the supply's actual regulated value). The PySpice plot in `tia_sweep.png` shows the same flat top.



For each R_F, the photocurrent at which the output first clips is



> **I_clip = V_DD / R_F**



| R_F      | I_clip      | Observed clipping in our data?                                  |

|----------|-------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|

| 1 MΩ     | **3.3 µA**  | Yes — clips under white cap, room light, *and* phone flashlight |

| 100 kΩ   | 33 µA       | Clips only under the phone flashlight                            |

| 10 kΩ    | 330 µA      | Output starts to soften (2.40 V) under the phone flashlight      |

| 1 kΩ     | 3.3 mA      | Never clips — phone flashlight only reaches 0.214 V              |



The 1 MΩ value saturates under almost any practical illumination because **3.3 µA is a tiny photocurrent for a BPW34 in normal indoor conditions.** The BPW34 has a responsivity of roughly 0.6 A/W at λ ≈ 850 nm and an active area of ~7.5 mm²; even a translucent white cap diffusing weak ambient light delivers more than enough irradiance to push the photocurrent past that 3.3 µA threshold. So the 1 MΩ feedback is really only useful for *very* dim signals (below a few microamps), and for everything brighter it operates as a saturation indicator rather than a quantitative measurement.



---



## 3. Linearity & Gain Validation (the success story)



The cleanest direct comparison the dataset offers is the **room-light column**, where neither the 100 kΩ nor the 10 kΩ row is clipped. The TIA equation predicts that swapping R_F by 10× should swap V_OUT by exactly 10×.



| R_F      | V_OUT (Room Light) | Implied I_photo = V_OUT / R_F |

|----------|-------------------:|-------------------------------:|

| 100 kΩ   | 1.342 V            | **13.42 µA**                  |

| 10 kΩ    | 0.1352 V           | **13.52 µA**                  |



Two observations:



- **Vout ratio:** 1.342 V / 0.1352 V = **9.93×**, against an ideal 10× — under 1 % error. This is a textbook validation of `V_OUT = I_photo · R_F`.

- **Two independent estimates of the same physical photocurrent** (room light hitting the photodiode) come out within 0.7 % of each other. The actual room photocurrent is therefore **≈ 13.5 µA**.



So inside the linear range the bench circuit and the PySpice prediction agree to within meter resolution. The simulation is doing its job — Microchip's macromodel, the diode parameters, and the ideal feedback math all line up against reality.



---



## 4. Non-Idealities — Why "Total Dark" Is Not 0 V



In simulation the BPW34 with `I_photo = 0` and an ideal op-amp gives `V_OUT = 0 V`. The breadboard does not. Look at the `Total Dark` column scaled by R_F:



| R_F      | V_OUT (Dark) | Apparent input current = V_OUT / R_F |

|----------|-------------:|-------------------------------------:|

| 1 MΩ     | 0.318 V      | 318 nA                               |

| 100 kΩ   | 24 mV        | 240 nA                               |

| 10 kΩ    | 3.1 mV       | 310 nA                               |

| 1 kΩ     | 5.8 mV       | 5800 nA *(see below)*                |



For the top three rows, **the implied input current is roughly the same — about 250–320 nA — over three decades of R_F.** That is the signature of a *real input current* being multiplied by R_F (just like the photocurrent). It is **not** a fixed voltage offset, because then it would scale with `(1 + R_F / R_pd)`, not directly with R_F. Plausible contributors, in order of likely magnitude:



- **Residual ambient light leakage.** "Total dark" on a breadboard is rarely truly dark — stray light through the room, the LED on the multimeter, even the phone screen, all reach the BPW34's 7.5 mm² window. A few hundred nanoamps corresponds to micro-watt-level irradiance; trivially achievable.

- **Photodiode dark current.** The BPW34 datasheet quotes ~2 nA at 10 V reverse bias; in our zero-bias photovoltaic configuration it is much smaller, but non-zero. This sets the floor *below* what we are seeing, so it is a contributor but not the dominant one.

- **Op-amp input bias current (I_b).** The MCP6004 is CMOS, so I_b ≈ 1 pA typical. Multiplied by 1 MΩ that is ≈ 1 µV — invisible at our resolution. Not the cause.



The **bottom row breaks the pattern**: at R_F = 1 kΩ the dark output is 5.8 mV, but `V_OUT = R_F · I_dark` would predict only ~0.3 mV. Two non-idealities take over once R_F is small enough that I_dark · R_F drops below them:



- **Op-amp input offset voltage (V_os).** The MCP6004 specifies ±2 mV typical (±4.5 mV max) at 25 °C. With the non-inverting input at GND, V_os shows up directly at the output (multiplied by the noise gain `1 + R_F/R_pd`, which here is essentially 1 because R_pd ≫ R_F). 5.8 mV sits comfortably inside the datasheet's max envelope.

- **Single-supply zero-input behavior.** Because the non-inverting input is at the negative rail (0 V), the output cannot swing below ground. Any negative offset gets clamped just above 0 V, biasing the apparent dark reading upward.



That `5.8 mV at 1 kΩ` is greater than `4.7 mV at 1 kΩ — White Cap` is itself diagnostic: at 1 kΩ we are no longer measuring photocurrent — we are measuring the op-amp's own offset floor.



---



## 5. Conclusion



The bench results align with the PySpice simulation exactly where they should and disagree exactly where the simulation's idealizations break down:



- **Slope (gain) — match.** Two independent R_F values give the same photocurrent estimate to better than 1 % (13.42 µA vs. 13.52 µA in room light), confirming `V_OUT = I_photo · R_F`.

- **Clipping ceiling — match.** Every saturated reading lands at 3.33 V, within meter accuracy of the simulated 3.3 V VDD rail. The 1 MΩ trace clips for everything brighter than total dark, exactly as predicted by `I_clip = 3.3 µA`.

- **Floor — diverges (as expected).** "Total dark" is nonzero on the bench. The dominant contributor at high R_F is residual ambient light (≈ 300 nA-equivalent of stray photocurrent); at low R_F (1 kΩ) the output is dominated by the MCP6004's input offset voltage (≈ 5–6 mV), not by photocurrent at all. Neither effect is in the PySpice model, which assumes an ideal `I_photo = 0` source and no V_os.



Together the data give a clean, quantitative validation of the TIA design: the simulation correctly predicts both the linear gain and the clipping behaviour; the residual offsets are real-world hardware effects with a well-understood, datasheet-traceable origin. The 1 MΩ feedback is best reserved for genuinely low-light applications, and any sub-millivolt measurement at small R_F should be interpreted relative to the op-amp's offset floor rather than as photocurrent.

How it works

OS integration. The primary backend uses ctypes to call DisplayServicesSetBrightness from /System/Library/PrivateFrameworks/DisplayServices.framework — the same private symbol the menu-bar slider and F1/F2 keys use. I went with raw ctypes instead of pyobjc because private C functions don't have Objective-C bridging metadata, so ctypes is actually the more stable path. If the framework can't be loaded (e.g. on a future macOS that locks it down), it falls back to the Homebrew brightness CLI.

Nits → slider math. macOS's brightness slider is approximately perceptually uniform, not luminance-linear. The forward model is

$$L(s) = L_{\max},s^{\gamma}$$

so to hit a target luminance $L_t$ you need

$$s = \left(\frac{L_t}{L_{\max}}\right)^{1/\gamma}.$$

I default to γ = 2.2, which matches the pure-power approximation of the sRGB transfer function and is the value most consistent with measurements people have published for Apple internal panels. The script also offers --curve srgb, which inverts the piecewise sRGB EOTF — linear below $L/L_{\max}=0.0031308$, then $1.055,(L/L_{\max})^{1/2.4}-0.055$ above — giving slightly better fidelity at the low end where the pure power law underestimates the slider position.

A naive linear nits/500 for 250 nits would set the slider to 0.50, which on a γ ≈ 2.2 panel actually outputs only ~109 nits — less than half the requested value. The 0.7297 the script produces is what you actually want.

Usage.

python set_nits.py 250 --curve srgb # use full sRGB EOTF

python set_nits.py 250 --dry-run # print slider value, don't apply

python set_nits.py 250 --gamma 2.4 # tune the curve to your panel

If you have a luminance meter, measure two or three points and fit your own γ via --gamma for best accuracy on this specific unit.

> . 解決「輸出卡在 0V」的底層限制：引入虛擬接地（Virtual Ground）

>

> 問題：目前 MCP6004 的非反相端（+）直接接地（0V），導致電壓無法出現負值。只要有一點負向的誤差，都會被硬生生卡在 0V 附近，導致低光照時的測量不準確。

>

> 改進方式：將非反相端（+）不要接 0V，而是接一個很小的參考電壓（例如利用分壓電阻分出 0.1V 或 VDD/2）。這樣你的輸出電壓就會是 $V_{OUT} = V_{ref} + (I_{photo} \times R_F)$。即使在全暗下，電壓也是停在 0.1V，你就可以精準觀測到雜訊在 0.1V 上下跳動，而不會被 0V 的地板吃掉。

  Pattern — keep R_F · C_F roughly constant



  For a fixed BW target the dominant pole 1/(2π·R_F·C_F) says C_F should scale as 1/R_F. The recommendations confirm this almost exactly: each time R_F drops 10×, the optimal C_F goes up

  roughly 10× (1 pF → 33 pF → 330 pF → 1 nF). The "knob" you're tuning is really the time constant τ = R_F · C_F ≈ 1 µs for ~24 kHz BW with margin.



  Two ends of the table behave specially



  - R_F = 1 MΩ is on a razor's edge. Only 1 pF clears the 20 kHz target — and the Bode plot shows that curve peaks above the flat gain (~+2 dB at 70 kHz), i.e. the loop is mildly

  under-compensated. The next size up (10 pF) already drops BW to 18.5 kHz, just below 20 kHz. With this op-amp (MCP6004 GBW ≈ 1 MHz) and the BPW34's 72 pF junction capacitance, 1 MΩ is

  essentially the largest gain at which 20 kHz detection is still feasible. Going to 10 MΩ would force a faster op-amp.

  - R_F = 1 kΩ has enormous headroom. Five of the seven C_F values still give > 1 MHz of bandwidth — they're op-amp-GBW limited, not RC limited. You can crank C_F up to 1 nF and still get 224

  kHz of bandwidth, which means at low gain you can buy aggressive out-of-band noise rejection essentially "for free." The only swept value that actually attenuates 20 kHz is 10 nF (-3 dB at

  16.5 kHz).



  Practical guidance



  If you're doing a single-gain design at one of these R_F values, the recommendations above are the picks. If you want a switchable-gain TIA where R_F changes (e.g., a programmable-gain front

   end), the cleanest approach is a per-R_F dedicated C_F matched in value, not a single C_F across all gains — otherwise you'll either lose 20 kHz at high gain or miss noise rejection at low

  gain.

有很多電容都會有底線 那是甚麼意思?

這是一個非常細心且專業的觀察！在電子零件的世界裡，這些微小的標記都藏著重要的規格密碼。

當你在陶瓷電容（就是我們剛剛說的那種圓扁豆狀的電容）上的數字（例如 104 或 221）底下看到一條橫線（底線）時，它通常代表的是「耐壓值（Working Voltage）」。

1. 陶瓷電容的「耐壓」密碼
早期和現在的許多陶瓷電容為了節省表面印刷的空間，會用這條底線來當作電壓的簡寫：

有底線（例如 104）：通常代表這顆電容的最大耐壓值是 50V 或 100V。這對於我們接在 3.3V 或 5V 的微控制器（MCU）和運算放大器電路來說，絕對綽綽有餘。

沒有底線（例如 104）：在傳統的標準中，如果沒有特別標示耐壓且沒有底線，通常暗示它的耐壓高達 500V。

2. 閱讀方向的防呆設計
除了標示耐壓值之外，這條底線有時候也兼具「防呆」的功能。就像我們看數字 68 或是 99 一樣，底線可以幫助工程師確認字體的閱讀方向，避免在生產線上把數值給看反了。

💡 額外補充：如果是「貼片電容」的橫線？
既然提到了橫線，未來你如果接觸到長方體的貼片電容（SMD），在零件邊緣出現的橫線意義就完全不同了，它代表的是極性（正負極）：

貼片鉭質電容（Tantalum）：有深色橫線或標記的那一端是正極（+）。

貼片鋁電解電容：有塗色或橫線的那一半邊是負極（-）。
(這點超級容易搞混，實驗室裡燒掉零件通常都是因為這個！)

總結來說，你手邊那些數值帶有底線的 10pF 或 22pF 陶瓷電容，代表它們是耐壓 50V 或 100V 的標準零件，你可以非常安心地把它們插進你的 3.3V TIA 電路中測試！

那333和332分別代表甚麼?

這是一個非常好的問題！這完全遵循我們之前提到「104 = 100nF」的同一套解碼邏輯。

在電子零件的標示中，這被稱為 EIA 三碼標示法。規則非常簡單：

第一、二位數字：代表有效數字。

第三位數字：代表要補上幾個「0」（也就是 10 的幾次方）。

算出來的預設單位：永遠都是 pF（皮法拉）。

拆解：前兩碼是 33，第三碼是 3（代表補上 3 個 0）。

數字：33,000 pF

單位換算：

= 33 nF (奈法拉，最常用的稱呼)

= 0.033 µF (微法拉)

2. 「332」代表什麼？
拆解：前兩碼是 33，第三碼是 2（代表補上 2 個 0）。

數字：3,300 pF

單位換算：

= 3.3 nF

= 0.0033 µF

第三碼是 3（如 333, 103）：就是 幾十 nF (333 = 33nF, 103 = 10nF)

第三碼是 2（如 332, 222）：就是 幾 nF 或是 幾千 pF (332 = 3.3nF = 3300pF)

第三碼是 1（如 101, 221）：就是 幾百 pF (101 = 100pF)

我現在遇到了另一個問題，因為螢幕閃爍，所以很常會有波峰在100k檔波谷在1M檔那我可以怎麼調整避免整個monitor都是換檔的黃線呢？

這是一個在儀器設計中最經典的進階問題！在工程界，這種現象被稱為「乒乓效應（Ping-Pong Effect）」或「抖動（Chattering）」。

因為你的光源（螢幕）是 120Hz 或 PWM 閃爍的，這代表光電流其實是一個「波浪」或「方波」。當波峰剛好超過 1MΩ 的極限（需要切換到 100kΩ），但波谷又掉回 1MΩ 的範圍時，Pico 的自動換檔邏輯就會被這個波浪帶著走，瘋狂上下換檔。

要解決這個問題，我們不需要改硬體，純粹靠修改 Pico 裡面的軟體（main.py）就能完美解決。業界通常有三種解法：

1. 引入「遲滯區間（Hysteresis）」
這是最標準的作法。我們不能讓「升檔」和「降檔」在同一個光亮度發生。

升檔（往 100k 切）：只要電壓一超過 3.1V，立刻切換到 100kΩ（因為不切換會削頂）。

降檔（往 1M 切）：切換到 100kΩ 後，電壓可能會掉到 0.31V。我們必須規定，除非電壓跌破 0.15V（甚至更低），否則絕對不准切回 1MΩ。這樣波谷就不會觸發降檔了。

2. 加入「降檔冷卻時間（Downshift Hold Time）」
當訊號是 120Hz 時，一個週期大約是 8 毫秒。如果我們規定「必須連續 50 毫秒都處於低電壓，才可以降檔」，那麼 Pico 就會完美忽略波谷，安穩地停在 100kΩ 檔位。

3. 加入「手動鎖檔（Manual Lock）」功能
這是最暴力但也最實用的功能！在儀表板加上 1、2、3、4 按鍵，讓你可以直接鎖定在 100kΩ 檔位測量，按 0 才回到自動換檔。
